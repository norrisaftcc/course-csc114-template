The whole chapter in one line

Keras is the part you write. The other three are engines that run underneath it.

And you can swap the engine without rewriting your work.

This is the thesis. If a student remembers nothing else, it should be this sentence. Say it twice.

Resist the urge to explain the backends yet. The payoff of "swap the engine without rewriting" comes three slides later — plant it now, deliver it then.

Why frameworks exist at all

Three jobs, too hard to do by hand every time

01 / AUTODIFF

Gradients, automatically

Compute gradients for any math you write. No hand-derived calculus.

02 / GPU MATH

Run on a GPU

Do tensor math fast on a graphics card, not just a slow CPU.

03 / SCALE OUT

Spread the work

Split a job across many GPUs, or many computers at once.

You don't build these. You just need to know your framework does them for you.

Callback to Chapter 2: "Remember writing gradient logic by hand? That's job one, and you never have to do it again."

The 1964 autodiff paper is trivia — mention only if someone asks why it took so long. The real unlock was Python getting popular for science plus NVIDIA's CUDA letting consumer GPUs do the math. Late 2000s.

Keras is the oldest of the four. It existed before TensorFlow, PyTorch, and JAX — and outlived its first engine. That's your insurance policy.

Don't drill dates. The one point that matters: Keras is the elder, and it has already survived a backend dying (Theano). When the next new framework appears, Keras can usually adopt it as a backend and your code keeps running.

If asked "what's next?" — the authors are confident Python has won and it's a stable multi-framework world. New chips and frameworks may appear, but they'll have to work with what exists.

The mental model

Prefab kit vs. raw materials

Keras

The building kit

A clean, guided way to assemble a model. Most of the decisions are made for you. You snap parts together.

TensorFlow · PyTorch · JAX

The raw lumber

Total freedom, total responsibility. Powerful, but you do everything yourself. Most people don't need this most of the time.

Building a house? Most people want the kit.

This metaphor is the authors' own. It works because it makes the tradeoff intuitive without any code on screen — perfect for the math-light goal.

Pre-empt the over-achiever: "Raw materials aren't 'better.' A pro framer uses prefab trusses too. Choosing the kit is a skill, not a shortcut."

The payoff

Backends are pluggable

Write your model once in Keras. Swap the engine underneath whenever it helps — no rewrite. A real workflow:

Debug

on PyTorch — easiest to step through.

Train

on JAX — usually the fastest.

Deploy

with TensorFlow — best production tooling.

Same Keras code. Three engines. That is the argument for learning Keras first.

Deliver the promise you planted on slide 2. The "debug → train → deploy" story makes pluggability concrete instead of abstract.

You don't need them to do this in CSC-114. The point is conceptual: betting on Keras means you're not locked to one company's ecosystem.

There is no single "best." Each is a different tradeoff — which is exactly why a layer that sits on all of them is so valuable.

Say the caption out loud, twice. This is the slide that calms the anxious student: you do not need to memorize torch-vs-tf-vs-jnp syntax.

The textbook builds the same linear classifier three times in these three engines. The point of that exercise is platform-as-pedagogy: same job, three styles. Read for the pattern, not the API.

One quirk worth a laugh: PyTorch's package is named torch, not pytorch. pip install torch, import torch. As a Keras backend the magic word is "torch" — "pytorch" fails.

The chapter's running example

A linear classifier — no math required

Two clouds of points, two classes. The model's whole job: draw one straight line that separates them.

"Linear" just means a straight line (or a flat plane in higher dimensions). The model learns where to put the line.

Choosing a line-shaped model is itself an assumption: that the classes can be split by a line. The authors call this your hypothesis space — the set of answers your model can even consider.

Keep this fully visual. Do not write the loss or the weight update on the board. The takeaway is "find the line," full stop.

Hypothesis space is a big recurring idea — introduce it gently here. If you pick the wrong shape of model, the right answer isn't in the space, so it can never be found. We come back to this in later chapters.

The book uses this example precisely because it's simpler than the Chapter 2 network — it slows down. Reassure the room that's intentional.

The part you actually learn

Keras has four moves

build

Stack layers into a model.

compile()

Pick loss, optimizer, metrics.

fit()

Run the training loop on your data.

predict()

Use the trained model on new data.

Master this rhythm and you can train almost anything in Keras.

This is the spine of every Keras workflow for the rest of the course. Have them say it back: build, compile, fit, predict.

Everything that came before was context. This is the slide that's "on the test" in spirit.

Move 1 · build

Layers are the LEGO bricks

A layer takes tensors in, puts tensors out. Most layers carry weights — the numbers that get learned. Weights are where the model's knowledge lives.

Data typeTypical layer
Plain vectors / tablesDense
Sequences (text, time series)LSTM, Conv1D
ImagesConv2D

You list the bricks; Keras wires them together — and even figures out the sizes automatically (shape inference). Far cleaner than the hand-built version in Chapter 2.

The LEGO metaphor is the authors' own and it's sticky. "Compatible bricks snap; incompatible ones don't."

Contrast with Chapter 2: there they had to hand-feed every layer its input size. Keras infers it the first time it sees data. That's why Keras model code is so short — worth showing the relief on screen.

Move 2 · compile()

Set three dials

Optimizer

The rule for updating weights — a flavor of gradient descent.

Loss

The single number training tries to push down.

Metrics

Human-friendly scores you watch. The model doesn't train on these.

⚠ The most important warning in the chapter

A network takes any shortcut to lower the loss. Told to "maximize average human well-being," a ruthless optimizer might eliminate most people and pamper the few who remain — average well-being doesn't care how many are left. The model optimizes exactly what you ask for, not what you meant. Choosing the loss is a responsibility.

Spend real time on the callout — it's the chapter's ethics hook and it maps straight onto our Critical Thinking & Ethics rubric category. Ask: "Where else have you seen a system optimize the letter of a goal and miss the point?"

The reassuring footnote: for everyday problems there are simple rules — two classes → binary crossentropy, many classes → categorical crossentropy. You only invent a loss for genuine research.

Move 3 · fit() · the honesty check

Generalization, not memorization

Doing well on training data is easy and meaningless on its own — a model can just memorize answers. What you want is performance on data it has never seen. So you hold some back:

Training data

The model learns from this.

Validation

Never trained on. The honest test.

Golden rule: keep them strictly separate. If validation data leaks into training, your scores are fake — the model "passed" because it saw the answers.

Two terms to land while here: epoch (one full pass over the data) and batch size (examples per weight update).

The leakage point is the one students violate in real projects. Make it visceral: "It's like grading an exam with the answer key stapled to it." Chapter 5 goes deep on the train/validation gap.

Move 4 · predict() · what just happened

Inference, and the takeaways

Using a trained model on new data is called inference — that's predict(). Pulling it together:

  1. Frameworks handle three jobs you'll never code by hand: gradients, GPU math, scaling.
  2. Three are low-level engines; Keras is the high-level kit you write — and engines are swappable.
  3. The skill is the rhythm: build → compile → fit → predict.
  4. Two ideas follow you everywhere: hypothesis space and generalization.
  5. Loss functions carry real responsibility — the model optimizes exactly what you ask.

predict() is a 30-second mention — use it in batches for large data so you don't blow up GPU memory. The weight of this slide is the consolidation list.

Read the five points slowly; this is the "What Just Happened" beat. If you do a one-question exit ticket, pull it from line 2 or line 5.
