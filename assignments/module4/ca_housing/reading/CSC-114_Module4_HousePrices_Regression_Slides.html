How Much Is
This House Worth?

Teaching a network to read facts about a neighborhood and guess a price.

Scalar regression · the California Housing dataset · Chollet & Watson, Ch. 4

Two kinds of question

Which bucket?
vs. How much?

Classification answers which category? Regression answers how much? — a continuous number, not a label. Our target: the median home price in a California district.

The data

Eight facts about
a neighborhood

Each data point is a district (a block group ≈ a neighborhood). We get 8 numbers describing it; we predict 1 number — the median home price. 1990 census; prices run ~$60k–$500k.

The catch

Tiny data.
480 to learn from.

480 training districts, 120 test. The small version is chosen on purpose — real datasets are often tiny, and you need to handle that.

california_housing.load_data(
  version="small")
# train (480, 8) · test (120, 8)
Preprocessing · 1 of 2

Put every feature
on the same scale

Features on different scales make learning hard. Fix: for each column, subtract its mean and divide by its standard deviation. Now every feature is centered at 0 with a similar spread.

(value − mean) / std
Preprocessing · the discipline

Mean & std come
from TRAIN only

Compute mean and std on the training data, then use those same numbers to normalize the test data. Never compute anything from the test set — even an average.

Preprocessing · 2 of 2

Shrink the
price too

Prices in the hundreds of thousands force the network to learn huge weights, which trains slowly. Divide every price by 100,000: $283,000 → 2.83. Multiply back at the end to read dollars.

The model

Small data → small model

Two hidden layers, 64 units each, then one output unit. Fewer samples → use a smaller model, or it memorizes instead of learning.

The key difference

The last layer is naked
(and that's the point)

A regression output must be free to land anywhere. Put no activation on the last layer — it stays linear. A sigmoid here would trap every prediction between 0 and 1.

layers.Dense(1) # no activation
The scoreboard

New scoreboard:
MSE & MAE

No "accuracy" for numbers. Train on mean squared error (squares the miss, so big misses hurt most). Watch mean absolute error — the average dollars you're off.

translate itMAE 0.5 ≈ off by $50,000 (because targets were ÷100,000).
★ The problem · part 1

The small-data
problem

A normal split leaves only ~100 districts to validate on. With so few, the score swings wildly depending on which 100 you happened to pick. You can't trust it.

★ The fix · K-fold cross-validation

K-fold: everyone
gets a turn

Split into 4 folds. Train 4 times; each time a different fold is the validation set, the other three are training. Average the 4 scores. Every district validates exactly once.

Why we bother

The four scores

Our folds scored 0.298, 0.349, 0.232, 0.305 — a real spread (off by $23k to $35k). Any single one would mislead. The average, 0.296 (~$29,600 off), is the number to trust.

How long to train?

Watch the curve

Average the per-epoch MAE across folds and plot it. It drops fast, flattens, then creeps back up. Improvement stalls around 120–140 epochs — past there, overfitting.

The final run

Train once at 130,
then grade once

Off by ~$31,000 against a $60k–$500k range — a solid first pass. That the test score lands near the K-fold estimate (0.296) means our validation was honest.

Using the model

Reading a
prediction

predict() returns a number: 2.83. Multiply by 100,000 → ~$283,000, the model's price guess for that district. No 0.5 cutoff, no class — the number is the answer.
