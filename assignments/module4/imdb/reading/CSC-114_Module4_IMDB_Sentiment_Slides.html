Positive or Negative?

Teaching a network to read a movie review
and guess how it felt.

Binary classification · the IMDB dataset · Chollet & Watson, Ch. 4

Welcome them in. Today we build the first real text classifier of the course. The whole arc: a computer can't read, so we'll turn words into numbers, stack three layers, and watch it learn — and then watch it overfit, which is the lesson that actually sticks. Tell them the one number to leave with: a model that gets better on its training data is not automatically a better model.

The job

Two buckets. One decision.

Read a review. Decide: positive or negative. That's binary classification — sorting each input into one of exactly two buckets.

"? this film was just brilliant casting location scenery story direction everyone…"

Review #0 — a person reads this and instantly knows. Our model has to learn to.

Anchor the running example here: review #0, which is labeled positive (1). We'll carry this exact review through the deck. Note the goal isn't a hard yes/no yet — it's a probability. A number between 0 and 1. Keep "two buckets" in their ears; the binary-classification recipe at the end all flows from "two."

The wall

A computer can't read. It does math on numbers.

So every review is delivered to us already turned into numbers — each integer stands for one word.

Review #0 — as words

this film was just brilliant …

Review #0 — as numbers

[1, 14, 22, 16, 43, 530, …]

Its label

0 = negative
1 = positive
Review #0 → 1

The IMDB dataset: 50,000 reviews, 25k train / 25k test, half positive half negative, and it ships inside Keras already converted to integer lists. Stress that the labels live in a separate list — features and target, the same vocabulary from Module 3. The features are the word-numbers; the target is the 0/1 label.

A detail that bites

Decoding: mind the shift of 3

To read a review we look up each number's word. But indices 0, 1, 2 are reserved for housekeeping — so every real word is shifted by 3.

Trap: forget the - 3 and your decoded reviews come out as nonsense. Your AI partner forgets this constantly — now you won't.
The leading "?" in the decoded review is the start token (index 1), which maps to no English word. This slide quietly plants Trap #3 for the assessment. If you have a minute, show the live decode in the notebook with and without the -3 to make the failure visceral.

A cheap, smart cut

Keep the top 10,000 words. Drop the rest.

The full vocabulary is over 88,000 words. Most appear in a single review — they can't teach a general pattern, and they'd make every review enormously wide.

from keras.datasets import imdb (train, _), (test, _) = imdb.load_data( num_words=10000)

Why it matters: manageable data size, almost no lost signal.

Plants Trap #5: students should be able to say WHY 10,000, not just parrot the number. Two reasons: rare words don't generalize, and capping the vocabulary keeps the encoded vectors a sane width (next slide). If an agent gives them the line without the reasoning, that's the tell.

Same shape for every review

Multi-hot encoding

Reviews differ in length; the network needs one fixed shape. So: a row of 10,000 slots, one per word. Put a 1 where a word is present, 0 everywhere else.

It loses word order and counts. It keeps which words appeared — enough to judge sentiment well.

This is the one genuinely new idea in the unit and the thing the assessment asks them to explain in their own words. Use the textbook's tiny example: the sequence [8,5] becomes a 10,000-long vector that is all zeros except 1s at positions 5 and 8. Emphasize what's thrown away (order, frequency) — surprising that this still works, which is the interesting part.

The model

Three layers, ending in one yes/no knob

Units = how much room to find patterns. relu = lets it learn non-straight-line patterns. 1 sigmoid unit = a single probability out.

Read it left to right as a pipeline. Don't over-explain relu — "lets the model bend, otherwise the whole stack collapses to one straight line" is enough at this stage; chapter 5 formalizes it. The part to burn in is the right end: one unit, sigmoid. That ending IS binary classification. Everything on the recipe card later hangs off it.

Why sigmoid

It squashes any number into 0–1

The output reads as a probability: near 1 → positive, near 0 → negative, near 0.5 → unsure.

layers.Dense(1, activation="sigmoid")

Keep this short and visual. The single takeaway: sigmoid turns the model's raw score into something we can read as a probability. That's why the final layer has exactly one unit and a sigmoid — and it's why the loss on the next slide is binary_crossentropy, which is built for probabilities.

How it learns it's wrong

The same loop from Module 3

binary_crossentropy punishes confident wrong answers hardest. adam is the reliable default optimizer. We also watch accuracy — but the model trains on the loss.

Tie it explicitly back to Module 3's training loop so it feels like return, not new material. The one nuance worth stating: loss vs metric. We minimize binary_crossentropy; accuracy is just a human-readable scoreboard we monitor. Students conflate the two constantly.

A trap that quietly ruins everything

Never tune on the test set

Tune against the validation set as much as you like. If you tune against the test set, you've let the model peek at the final exam — its score stops meaning anything.

x_val = x_train[:10000] partial = x_train[10000:]

Plants Trap #4. The intuition that lands: the test set is a final exam you only get to sit once. Every time you peek and adjust, you've turned it into a practice test. Three piles, three jobs — keep them straight. The validation set is what makes the next slide (overfitting) observable at all.

The most important picture in this unit

Watch it overfit

training loss — keeps dropping validation loss — dips, then climbs

Better on the practice test, worse on the real exam. After ~epoch 4 the model memorizes quirks that don't carry to new reviews.

Trap: "more epochs = better." This curve is the counter-evidence.
Spend real time here — this is the slide they should leave with in their heads. Walk the two lines separately: training loss falls forever (it's just getting better at memorizing what it's already seen); validation loss is the honest signal, and it turns around. The exact turnaround epoch wanders run to run — teach the SHAPE, not "4." This is also the anchor for the graded assessment: students will point at their OWN run's turnaround and explain it.

The fix

Retrain for ~4 epochs, then grade once

# fresh model, stop at the peak model.fit(x_train, y_train, epochs=4, batch_size=512) model.evaluate(x_test, y_test)

~88% from a few lines. Your number wobbles a little each run — random start, expected.

The fix is blunt: throw away the overtrained model, train a fresh one only to the peak, evaluate once on the sealed test set. ~88% is the honest number for this naive approach; ~95% is what polished methods reach. Use the wobble to pre-empt the inevitable "I got 0.87, is that wrong?" — no, that's random initialization, it's fine.

Using the trained model

Predictions are probabilities

predict()read as
0.98very sure: positive
0.99very sure: positive
0.02very sure: negative
0.65leaning positive, unsure
0.41leaning negative, unsure

A confidently positive review like #0 scores up near 1. Some land near 0.5 — the model is honestly on the fence, the way a person is about a mixed review.

That uncertainty isn't a bug. It's the model declining to fake confidence.

Close the loop on the running review: #0 was positive, and a confident model scores reviews like it near 1. (Don't claim a specific predicted value for #0 — predict() in the book runs on the test set, a different review.) The 0.5-ish outputs are worth dwelling on for the ethics thread: a model that outputs honest uncertainty is more trustworthy than one that always sounds sure.

Catch the AI: this is a two-bucket problem. If an agent reaches for softmax or categorical_crossentropy, it's confusing this with a many-class problem — wrong tool.
This is the keeper slide — tell them to photograph it. Then plant the headline trap (Trap #1) explicitly: softmax and categorical_crossentropy are for many mutually-exclusive classes; binary is one sigmoid unit + binary_crossentropy. Because we deliberately never taught the multiclass case, an agent bleeding the two together is exactly the kind of confident error the assessment rewards them for catching.
